Section A • 5 marks

Core concepts and skills

This section is short and direct. You should be ready to define or distinguish key early-semester concepts.

Know these especially well:

  • Broad topic vs research question
  • Primary source vs secondary source
  • Policy brief vs academic essay
  • Correlation vs causation
  • One feature of a good chart/graph
What kind of answer gets full marks here?

Usually a short, precise answer. Do not over-write. If asked for a difference, clearly state both sides. If asked for an example, give a concrete one.

Section B • 3 marks

Data interpretation

This section tests whether you can read evidence carefully and avoid making exaggerated claims.

What to remember

  • Describe what the data actually shows.
  • Do not claim more than the evidence proves.
  • Separate observation from explanation.
  • If data shows a pattern, that does not automatically prove the reason for the pattern.
Safe formula: “A valid conclusion is X. However, the evidence does not by itself prove Y, because other explanations may also exist.”
Section C • 8 marks

Campaign strategy and communication

This is one of the most important parts of the exam. You need to show that you understand how a campaign is designed strategically.

Be ready to do all of these:

  • Identify an appropriate target audience
  • Define a clear campaign goal
  • Write or explain a key message
  • Choose outputs that fit the audience
  • Explain how logos, ethos, and pathos would be used

Audience

Be specific. “Students” may be too broad unless you explain which students and why.

Outputs

Do not choose randomly. Explain why this format fits the audience and goal.

Rhetoric

Do not just define logos/ethos/pathos. Show how they would function in the campaign.

Quick reminder: what makes a strong campaign answer?
  • Clear audience
  • Clear desired change or action
  • Strong message
  • Outputs that make sense
  • Deliberate rhetorical strategy
Section D • 9 marks

Model United Nations strategy and resolution work

This is the heaviest section on the exam. You need to show that you understand how a delegation thinks and acts strategically.

Be ready to explain:

  • Delegation priorities
  • Likely allies
  • What wording or proposals would be resisted
  • What kinds of operative clauses a delegation would support

Core MUN ideas to remember

interests red lines allies opponents negotiation compromise operative clause diplomatic wording
Common trap: Students often answer from their personal opinion. On MUN questions, answer from the delegation’s likely position and strategic logic.
What does a good operative clause usually include?
  • An operative verb: Encourages, Calls upon, Urges, Requests
  • An actor: Member States, UN bodies, donor states
  • An action: funding, coordination, technical support, etc.
  • Realistic diplomatic wording
Quick Review

Key Concepts

Research question
A specific, answerable question that guides inquiry.
Primary source
Original material such as interviews, speeches, treaties, survey data, or official documents.
Secondary source
Analysis written by others, such as academic books or journal articles.
Policy brief
Short, direct, practical writing aimed at decision-makers and recommendations.
Correlation
Two things vary together; this alone does not prove one causes the other.
Target audience
The specific group a campaign is trying to reach and persuade.
Logos
Appeal through logic, evidence, or reasoning.
Ethos
Appeal through credibility, legitimacy, or trustworthiness.
Pathos
Appeal through emotion, values, concern, or identification.
Red line
A proposal, wording, or commitment a delegation will not accept.
Operative clause
The action part of a resolution: what states or bodies are encouraged or asked to do.
Allies
Delegations whose interests or policy preferences are close enough for cooperation.
Avoid These

Common mistakes

  • Giving a topic instead of a research question
  • Confusing attendance decline with proof of declining interest
  • Saying a campaign is for “everyone”
  • Choosing outputs without explaining why they fit the audience
  • Defining logos/ethos/pathos without applying them to the scenario
  • Answering MUN questions from personal opinion instead of delegation logic
  • Writing an “operative clause” that sounds like an essay sentence instead of resolution language
Practice

Practice questions

Practice 1 – Core concepts

Question: Explain the difference between an academic essay and a policy brief.

Model answer

An academic essay is usually more analytical, detailed, and aimed at explaining or evaluating an issue in depth. A policy brief is shorter, more direct, and designed for decision-makers, usually with practical recommendations.

Practice 2 – Data interpretation

Question: A chart shows that student club attendance fell over four weeks. What can you say safely, and what can you not claim for sure?

Model answer

You can say attendance fell over time. You cannot say for certain why it fell without more evidence, because other explanations may exist.

Practice 3 – Campaign strategy

Question: A campaign wants more students to use public transport to campus. Name a likely audience, one good output, and one rhetorical appeal that would work well.

Model answer

A likely audience could be commuting students. A strong output could be an Instagram carousel or campus poster. A useful rhetorical appeal could be logos through cost savings and convenience, or pathos through stress reduction and environmental responsibility.

Practice 4 – MUN strategy

Question: A delegation supports host-state assistance but resists unrealistic obligations. What kind of clause would it likely support?

Model answer

It would likely support an operative clause encouraging Member States to provide financial and technical support to heavily affected host countries while respecting national capacity and practical implementation constraints.
