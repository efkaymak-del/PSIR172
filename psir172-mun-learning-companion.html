UN & MUN Basics

Big idea: In Model United Nations, you do not speak as yourself. You represent a country, defend that country’s interests, and try to negotiate realistic outcomes.

What is the United Nations?

The United Nations (UN) is an international organization founded in 1945. It brings states together to address international peace and security, development, human rights, humanitarian crises, and international cooperation.

In simple terms: it is one of the main global forums where states debate problems, negotiate responses, and coordinate action.

What is Model UN?

Model UN (MUN) is a classroom or conference simulation of UN-style diplomacy. Students represent states, research positions, deliver speeches, negotiate with other delegates, and draft documents such as resolutions.

The point is not just public speaking. It is also about research, strategic thinking, negotiation, and understanding how international politics works in practice.

The UN’s six principal organs

Body What it does Why it matters for MUN
General Assembly (GA) All member states participate; discusses a wide range of international issues. Most classroom MUNs use GA-style bodies because they allow broad participation.
Security Council (UNSC) Focuses on international peace and security; smaller membership; can act more directly. Popular in MUN, but often too small and restrictive for large classes.
ECOSOC Coordinates work on economic, social, and development issues. Good for issue-based and development-oriented simulations.
International Court of Justice (ICJ) Settles legal disputes between states and issues advisory opinions. Some MUNs simulate legal argument rather than committee debate.
Secretariat Administrative arm of the UN, led by the Secretary-General. In some simulations, staff or crisis updates imitate secretariat functions.
Trusteeship Council Historically oversaw trust territories; now inactive. Mostly part of UN background rather than common simulation practice.

Common MUN formats worldwide

General Assembly Security Council ECOSOC Regional Bodies Specialized Agencies Crisis Committees

Different formats produce different classroom dynamics. Large bodies encourage broad participation; smaller bodies may be more intense and competitive; crisis bodies move faster and often require improvisation.

Why we are not doing UNSC

  • The real Security Council has only 15 members.
  • Our class is much larger, so UNSC would exclude too many students.
  • A General Assembly-style format is easier for first-year students.
  • GA-style debate suits a humanitarian topic like refugees and displacement.

Our Class Committee

Our design: one full-class, single-room, General Assembly-style humanitarian committee on International Responsibility-Sharing for Refugees and Displaced Persons.

Why this format fits our class

  • Everyone participates in the same room.
  • Each delegation has two students, so responsibilities can be shared.
  • The topic is broad enough to generate different national positions.
  • Students can research the issue with accessible sources.

What each delegation does

  • Research the assigned country
  • Identify national interests and likely policy positions
  • Write a position paper
  • Prepare an opening speech
  • Negotiate with other delegations
  • Help draft or amend a resolution

How this simulation differs from a real UN committee

Real UN Our classroom MUN
Long negotiations across many meetings Compressed into a few class sessions
Formal institutional procedures and long document chains Simplified rules for clarity and time management
Professional diplomats and state officials Students learning diplomatic role-play
Large staffs and extensive resources Research using class tools and public sources
Important: this is not a test of whether you personally agree with your country. It is a test of whether you can understand and represent a state position in a diplomatic setting.

How MUN Works: Step by Step

The basic flow

  1. Get assigned a country and learn its role in the issue.
  2. Research its interests, concerns, and likely policy positions.
  3. Write a position paper summarizing what your country wants.
  4. Prepare an opening speech that states your position clearly.
  5. Participate in formal debate when recognized by the chair.
  6. Caucus informally with other delegates to form alliances or blocs.
  7. Draft clauses and negotiate wording for a resolution.
  8. Debate and revise draft language.
  9. Vote or seek consensus on final text.

Before committee

  • Learn the issue
  • Learn the country
  • Map allies and tensions
  • Prepare your speech
  • Know your red lines

During formal session

  • Speak when recognized
  • Be concise
  • Stay in character
  • Ask what action is realistic
  • Listen for possible allies

During caucusing

  • Find common ground
  • Compare priorities
  • Trade wording
  • Build coalitions
  • Draft usable clauses

What is caucusing?

Caucusing is the less formal phase of committee when delegates move around, talk directly, compare ideas, and try to build support. This is often where the real political work happens.

In other words: formal speeches show positions, but caucusing often produces deals.

What does “bloc formation” mean?

A bloc is a loose grouping of countries with similar concerns or overlapping goals. For example, host states may want more burden-sharing, donor states may push funding mechanisms, and sovereignty-focused states may resist overly intrusive language.

What makes a strong delegate?

  • Knows the country’s position
  • Speaks clearly and briefly
  • Offers realistic proposals
  • Negotiates rather than just repeats moral claims
  • Can compromise on wording without losing core interests

Writing Support: Position Papers, Speeches, and Resolutions

Position paper: what it should do

  • Explain the issue briefly
  • State your country’s position
  • Identify interests and concerns
  • Show what your delegation supports
  • Indicate what your delegation rejects
  • End with realistic proposals
Think of it like this: your position paper is your delegation’s roadmap for committee.

Opening speech: what it should do

  • Name the problem
  • Show why it matters to your country
  • State your country’s principle or concern
  • Call for one or two realistic actions

Aim for about 45–75 seconds.

How resolutions work

A resolution usually has two major parts:

  • Preambulatory clauses – background, principles, concerns, context
  • Operative clauses – actions the committee recommends, requests, encourages, or supports

Preambulatory clause starters

Recognizing Acknowledging Deeply concerned Noting with concern Recalling Affirming Emphasizing Guided by

These set the context. They do not tell states what to do. They explain why the issue matters.

Operative clause starters

Calls upon Encourages Recommends Requests Supports Urges Invites Further requests

These are the action clauses. They say what the committee wants to happen.

Mini example

Type Example
Preambulatory Recognizing the disproportionate pressure placed on major refugee-hosting states,
Operative Encourages member states to increase financial and technical support for countries hosting large refugee populations,

Strong diplomatic phrasing

  • The delegation of ___ emphasizes…
  • Our delegation recognizes…
  • We stress the importance of…
  • We encourage member states to…
  • We remain concerned about…
  • We support practical cooperation on…
  • We call for balanced responsibility-sharing…
  • We welcome constructive dialogue…

Weak habits to avoid

  • Speaking only from personal opinion
  • Using very emotional but vague language
  • Saying “we should just solve it” without a mechanism
  • Ignoring sovereignty and political constraints
  • Proposing unrealistic or impossible solutions
Rule of thumb: good MUN writing is not just morally expressive. It is politically realistic, specific, and state-centered.

Protocol & Survival Guide

Basic rules for students

  1. Speak as your assigned country, not as yourself.
  2. Wait to be recognized before speaking in formal session.
  3. Be brief and clear.
  4. Use diplomatic language even when disagreeing.
  5. Negotiate actively during caucusing.
  6. Try to improve wording, not just repeat positions.

What common phrases mean

  • “The Chair recognizes…” = you may speak now.
  • “Motion” = a formal proposal for how committee should proceed.
  • “Caucus” = a structured or informal discussion phase.
  • “Amendment” = a proposed change to draft text.
  • “Yield” = giving up or transferring remaining speaking time, where allowed.

How to disagree diplomatically

Less effective Better diplomatic version
That idea is wrong. Our delegation has reservations about that proposal.
We reject that completely. Our delegation would have difficulty supporting that language in its current form.
You are being unrealistic. Our delegation believes a more practical approach would be…
That violates our interests. Our delegation is concerned that such a measure may conflict with national capacity or sovereignty considerations.

If you feel nervous

  • Use your position paper as your anchor.
  • Keep your opening speech short and prepared.
  • During caucus, ask questions before making big statements.
  • You do not need to sound like a professional diplomat.
  • You do need to sound prepared, respectful, and clear.

If you get stuck

  • Return to your country’s interests.
  • Ask: what would our delegation realistically support?
  • Ask another delegation: what language could we both accept?
  • Propose a narrower, more practical clause.
Do not panic if you do not know everything. No delegation knows every detail. The goal is not perfection. The goal is informed, diplomatic participation.

FAQ

What if I personally disagree with my country?

That is normal. In MUN you are not defending your own beliefs. You are practicing political representation, role-play, and diplomatic analysis.

What if I am shy or uncomfortable speaking?

Prepare a short opening speech in advance, speak clearly, and keep it simple. Also remember that MUN is not only about speeches; research, drafting, and negotiation matter too.

What if I do not know my country perfectly?

No one knows everything. Focus on the agenda, your country’s basic interests, and a few realistic positions it would likely support or oppose.

Do I need to sound extremely formal?

No. You should be respectful and diplomatic, but you do not need to sound artificial. Clear and professional is better than exaggerated imitation.

What if our delegation is unsure about a clause?

Ask whether the wording is realistic, whether it fits your country’s interests, and whether it could gain support from others. Good MUN often depends on wording changes.

What if I say something incorrect?

Correct yourself, refine your point, or shift to a more general position. Committee is about problem-solving and adaptation, not perfection.
