Target Audience Analysis

Transition slide. Move quickly to the next one.

Know Your Audience

Every message is for someone specific.

1. What do they already believe?

You're not starting from zero. Work with their existing views — or deliberately challenge them.

2. What do they care about?

A finance minister cares about costs. An NGO director cares about rights. Frame around their priorities.

3. What would move them?

Information alone rarely works. You need an emotional hook that connects evidence to action.

Interactive moment: "Who is your policy brief addressed to? Give me an example." Take 2-3 answers. For each, ask: "What does that person care about most — money, votes, lives, reputation?" 5 minutes on this slide.

Concept 2 of 3

Emotional Framing

Ethos · Pathos · Logos

Transition. Move to next slide.

The Three Engines of Persuasion

Ethos — Credibility

Why should I listen to you?

Credentials, authoritative sources, demonstrated expertise.

Pathos — Emotion

Why should I care?

Vivid examples, human stories, striking data. The hook that gets people to read past the first paragraph.

Logos — Evidence

Why should I believe you?

Data, research, cause-and-effect reasoning.

5 minutes. Emphasise: a good policy brief uses all three, but pathos is the hook. Move to next slide for the killer example.

Same Crisis. Two Sentences.

"200,000 refugees crossed the Mediterranean last year."

Logos

"A three-year-old boy washed up on a beach in Turkey."

Pathos

One of these changed European policy overnight.

That's framing.

Let this sink in for a moment. The Alan Kurdi photo (2015) shifted European migration debate more than years of statistics. This is what framing does. Both are true. Neither is lying. But one moves people to act.

Concept 3 of 3

Positive vs. Negative Framing

Transition. This is the most directly useful concept for their briefs.

Same Policy, Two Angles

Positive Frame

Emotion: Hope, opportunity

Logic: "If we do X, we gain Y"

Best for: Inspiring action, building coalitions

"Investing in renewable energy will create 50,000 new jobs."

Negative Frame

Emotion: Fear, urgency

Logic: "If we don't do X, we lose Y"

Best for: Mobilising against threats

"If we don't act on climate change, coastal cities will flood within 30 years."

7 minutes on this slide. Key insight: negative frames grab attention faster, but positive frames sustain engagement. Good briefs use BOTH: negative for the problem, positive for the recommendation. Write the table on the board if needed.

The Rule for Your Policy Brief

Problem → negative frame

Recommendation → positive frame

Scare them into reading. Inspire them into acting.

Simple takeaway. This directly maps to their brief structure. Pause, let them absorb it, then move to the framing experiment.

The Message Box

How campaigns think about messaging

What WE say about US
What WE say about THEM
What THEY say about THEM
"We promote clean energy and jobs"
"They ignore environmental costs"
What THEY say about US
"They call us idealistic — but the evidence supports us"
"They claim to support workers but oppose renewable investment"

Your group project has an opponent — not a person, but a position or status quo. Fill this in and you'll know your messaging strategy.

Walk through the example. Emphasise: "opponent" doesn't have to be a person — it can be the status quo, a competing policy, public apathy. The box forces you to anticipate the counter-argument, which makes your campaign stronger.

Three Rules of Ethical Persuasion

1 Don't fabricate evidence.

2 Don't impersonate or misrepresent.

3 If you'd be embarrassed to explain your method publicly, don't use it.

Quick — 2 minutes. The group project includes an ethics self-assessment in the app. Take it seriously. These rules apply to their briefs too.
