Clip 1 "Hello Türkiye" — Nation Rebranding (2022)

Context: Turkey's Communications Directorate launched this campaign to get the world to use "Türkiye" instead of "Turkey." The UN accepted the change in June 2022.

Turkish citizens International media Tourists Diplomats / UN All of the above
Positive Negative Both
Ethos Pathos Logos
Paid Earned Owned Owned → Earned

Analysis: "Hello Türkiye"

Target Audience: International media, diplomats, and the global public — NOT Turkish citizens. The campaign was in English and aimed outward.

Framing: Positive — national pride, modernity, identity. No attack on anyone; purely aspirational.

Persuasion: Ethos (government authority, official UN process) + Pathos (national pride, emotional music, diverse voices saying "Hello Türkiye").

Medium: Owned (official government video) → Generated massive earned media because the name change itself was newsworthy. Global news covered it for free.

Key lesson: If you make the message itself the story, earned media follows without a big advertising budget.

Clip 2 Yeşilay "Their Gain, Our Loss" — Anti-Tobacco (2019)

Context: Produced by the Turkish Green Crescent (Yeşilay) with Vital Strategies. Turkey has one of Europe's highest smoking rates. Tobacco kills ~300 people per day in Turkey.

Turkish smokers Young people Policymakers General public
Positive Negative Both
Ethos Pathos Logos
Smokers The tobacco industry The government No clear villain

Analysis: Yeşilay Anti-Tobacco

Target Audience: Turkish smokers and the general public, but also policymakers (supporting continued regulation).

Framing: Strongly negative — loss, death, exploitation. "Their gain" = industry profits; "our loss" = Turkish lives.

Persuasion: Pathos (suffering, death imagery) + Logos (300 deaths/day, industry profit data) + Ethos (Yeşilay is a century-old institution).

Villain: The tobacco industry — framed as profiting from Turkish deaths. This "villain framing" is a classic negative-campaign technique: give the audience someone to blame.

Ethics question: Is fear-based health messaging manipulation or justified public health communication? The line is debatable. Yeşilay stays on the right side because they use real data, not fabricated emotion.

Clip 3 North Cyprus Tourism Ad — UK Controversy (2022)

Context: The North Cyprus Tourist Board placed this ad on ITV in the UK. It became the 3rd most complained-about ad in Britain in 2022 — 394 complaints to the Advertising Standards Authority. Greek Cypriot diaspora organisations argued it promoted tourism to occupied territory. The ASA investigated and ruled the ad did not break any advertising rules.

British holidaymakers Turkish Cypriots Greek Cypriots International diplomats
British holidaymakers Greek Cypriot diaspora Turkish media No one
It was banned It was ignored Complaints amplified it It went viral for the wrong reasons

Analysis: TRNC Tourism Ad

Intended Audience: British holidaymakers looking for a Mediterranean destination. The ad was a straightforward tourism promotion — sun, sea, culture.

Actual Respondents: Greek Cypriot diaspora organisations in the UK, who viewed it as promoting tourism to occupied territory. 394 formal complaints.

Framing: Positive — relaxation, natural beauty, cultural heritage. There was nothing aggressive or political in the ad itself.

Outcome: The ASA ruled no rules were broken. But the complaints generated significant news coverage — articles in national UK media, social media debate, even parliamentary discussion. The ad arguably reached MORE people because of the controversy than it would have through its paid TV slot alone.

Key lesson: Your target audience is not the only audience. Campaign strategy must account for hostile audiences who may respond to your message in ways you didn't intend. The complaints created a "Streisand effect" — trying to suppress the ad made it more visible.

Clip 4 Nike "Dream Crazy" / Kaepernick (2018)

Context: Nike featured Colin Kaepernick — an American football player who knelt during the national anthem to protest police brutality — as the face of their "Just Do It" anniversary campaign. Some consumers burned their Nikes in protest. Nike's stock rose.

All Americans Young, diverse, progressive consumers Conservative sports fans Athletes only
Positive Negative Both
Ethos Pathos Logos

Analysis: Nike "Dream Crazy"

Target Audience: Young, diverse, progressive consumers — specifically NOT conservative older sports fans. Nike deliberately chose their future customer base over their current one.

Framing: Positive — aspiration, courage, sacrifice, defiance. "Believe in something, even if it means sacrificing everything."

Persuasion: Pathos (emotional storytelling, inspiring music, underdog narratives) + Ethos (Kaepernick's personal sacrifice gives him credibility on the topic of standing up for beliefs).

Medium: Paid (TV/digital ad) → generated enormous earned media. The controversy — people burning Nikes, politicians condemning it — became the story. Nike got billions in free media coverage.

Key lesson: If you try to speak to everyone, you speak to no one. Nike knew exactly who their audience was AND who it wasn't. The backlash was part of the strategy.

Key Concepts: Campaign Strategy

Reference notes from today's mini-lecture. Use these for your group project and midterm revision.

Professionalisation of Campaigns

McNair's key argument (Ch 6–7): political communication has become a professional industry. Modern campaigns use pollsters, media consultants, data analysts, and PR strategists. Communication is planned, tested, and disciplined — not improvised. Even "spontaneous" moments are often scripted.

For your group project: You don't need a big budget. But you DO need a clear strategy before you choose a medium. Message → Audience → Goal → Medium → Tool.

Paid vs. Earned vs. Owned Media

TypeDefinitionExample
PaidYou buy placement. Full message control, but audience knows it's an ad.TV ads, sponsored social posts, billboards
EarnedOthers tell your story. Most credible, but you can't control it.News coverage, social media shares, word of mouth
OwnedYour platforms. You control message + timing, but limited reach.Website, social accounts, newsletter, app
The sweet spot: Use owned media to create something newsworthy → earn media coverage → amplify with paid if needed.

Negative Campaigning

TypeMethodRisk
Direct AttackTarget the opponent's record with factsCan seem aggressive
ContrastCompare your position vs. theirsSafest form
Fear AppealEmotional threat, often without specific evidenceHighest boomerang risk
Boomerang effect: If the attack seems unfair, people sympathise with the target and turn against the attacker.

Message Discipline

Everyone on the campaign team says the same thing, the same way, every time. If a journalist asks five people the same question, they give the same answer. Inconsistency confuses audiences and undermines trust.

Ethics of Persuasion — Three Rules

1. Don't fabricate evidence.
2. Don't impersonate or misrepresent.
3. If you'd be embarrassed to explain your method publicly, don't use it.

Practice: Section C

This is the same format as the exam. Write your answer before looking at the model answer. Time yourself — aim for 15–20 minutes.

Scenario: A local animal shelter is struggling. Fewer people are adopting pets, and the shelter is running out of space and funding. The shelter director has asked you to design a short communication campaign to encourage more adoptions.

(a) Target Audience (2 marks)

Who is your campaign aimed at? Choose a specific group and explain why.

(b) Framing Strategy (2 marks)

Positive, negative, or both? Explain your choice and give an example message.

(c) Key Message (2 marks)

Write 3–4 sentences for a poster, social post, or video. Use at least 2 persuasion modes.

(d) Ethical Awareness (2 marks)

Identify one ethical risk and explain how you would avoid it.

Model Answer

(a) Target Audience: Young adults aged 20–30 living near the shelter, especially university students and young professionals living alone or in shared housing. This group is more likely to have flexible schedules and living situations that allow pet ownership, and they are heavy social media users, making them easy to reach online.

(b) Framing Strategy: Both. Open with negative framing to create urgency: "Every week, the shelter takes in more animals than it can hold. Without new adoptions, healthy animals face being put down." Then shift to positive framing for the solution: "One visit could change two lives — yours and theirs." The negative frame establishes the problem; the positive frame makes the action feel rewarding rather than burdensome.

(c) Key Message: "Right now, 47 dogs and cats at the Famagusta City Shelter are waiting for a home. Each one is healthy, vaccinated, and ready to be part of your family. Dr. Ayşe Polat, the shelter's head veterinarian, says: 'These animals have so much love to give — they just need someone to give them a chance.' Visit us this Saturday. Your new best friend is already here."

This uses logos (47 animals, vaccinated, healthy — concrete facts), ethos (Dr. Polat, head veterinarian — expert credibility), and pathos ("so much love to give," "your new best friend" — emotional connection).

(d) Ethical Risk: The campaign could use guilt-tripping language ("these animals will die because of you") or show distressing images of suffering animals to manipulate emotions. This crosses the line from persuasion into manipulation. To avoid it, I would focus on hopeful, positive images of healthy animals and use factual urgency (shelter capacity numbers) rather than graphic emotional pressure. The message should motivate action through hope, not guilt.
