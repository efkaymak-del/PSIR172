UN & MUN Basics

Main idea: in Model United Nations, you represent a country rather than yourself. Your task is to understand that country’s interests and negotiate realistic outcomes.

What is the United Nations?

The United Nations is an international organization founded in 1945. It provides a forum where states discuss international peace and security, humanitarian issues, development, law, and human rights.

In practical terms, it is one of the main places where governments communicate, debate, and coordinate action.

What is Model UN?

Model UN is a simulation of international diplomacy. Students represent states, research their country’s position, deliver speeches, negotiate with other delegates, and help draft committee documents.

MUN is about public speaking, but also about research, negotiation, strategy, and learning how politics works under real constraints.

The UN’s principal organs

Body Function MUN relevance
General Assembly All member states participate; discusses broad international issues. Most suitable for large educational simulations.
Security Council Focuses on international peace and security; smaller membership. Popular in MUN, but too small for a large full-class simulation.
ECOSOC Focuses on economic, social, and development issues. Common in issue-based MUN formats.
International Court of Justice Settles legal disputes and gives advisory opinions. Relevant in legal-style or special MUN simulations.
Secretariat Administrative body led by the Secretary-General. Sometimes simulated indirectly through staff roles.
Trusteeship Council Historically oversaw trust territories; now inactive. Mainly background knowledge for UN structure.

Common MUN committee formats

General Assembly Security Council ECOSOC Regional Bodies Specialized Agencies Crisis Committees

Different committee types create different kinds of debate. Large committees usually emphasize broad participation and coalition-building. Smaller crisis-style committees emphasize speed and improvisation.

Why we are using a GA-style format

  • Our class is too large for a realistic UNSC simulation.
  • We want everyone participating in one room.
  • A humanitarian topic fits a General Assembly-style committee well.
  • This format is more accessible for first-year students.

Our Simulation

Our format: one full-class Model UN simulation in a single room, with 18 delegations and 2 students per delegation.

Agenda

International Responsibility-Sharing for Refugees and Displaced Persons

This topic works well because countries approach it from different positions: host states, donor states, transit states, destination states, and major powers.

Delegation tasks

  • Research the assigned country
  • Identify its interests and concerns
  • Write a position paper
  • Prepare an opening speech
  • Negotiate during caucusing
  • Help draft a resolution

Simple sequence of the simulation

Stage What happens
Preparation Students research country positions and prepare position papers and speeches.
Opening session The chair opens committee; delegates introduce positions through opening speeches.
Moderated discussion Delegates make short formal interventions on key sub-issues.
Caucusing Delegates negotiate informally, form blocs, and test ideas.
Drafting Countries work on clauses and potential resolution language.
Negotiation & amendment Delegates revise wording and try to make proposals more acceptable.
Final decision The committee moves toward consensus or a vote on the final text.
Important: the point is not to say what you personally think is morally best. The point is to represent a state position in a diplomatic environment.

Position Papers

What a position paper does

  • Explains the issue briefly
  • States your country’s overall position
  • Identifies national interests and concerns
  • Shows what your delegation supports
  • Shows what your delegation would reject
  • Proposes realistic policy directions

A simple structure

  1. Issue overview
  2. Country position
  3. National interests / priorities
  4. Policies supported
  5. Red lines / limits
  6. Conclusion

Questions to guide your writing

  • Is your country mainly a host, donor, transit, or destination state?
  • What pressures does the issue create for your country?
  • What policies has your country previously supported?
  • What burden-sharing arrangement would your country accept?
  • What proposals would your country probably oppose?
Common mistake: writing a personal essay about what should happen. A position paper is not your personal opinion. It is the position of your delegation.

Opening Speeches

What your opening speech should do

  • Name the problem
  • Explain why the issue matters to your country
  • State your country’s position clearly
  • Call for one or two realistic actions

Aim for about 45–75 seconds.

Simple speech structure

  1. Formal opening
  2. Short issue statement
  3. Country concern or principle
  4. Supported action
  5. Diplomatic close

Useful diplomatic phrases

The delegation of ___ emphasizes... Our delegation remains concerned by... We support practical cooperation on... We encourage member states to... We call for balanced responsibility-sharing... We welcome constructive dialogue...
Tip: do not try to sound theatrical. Clear, short, and confident is better than overdramatic.

Resolutions & Clauses

What is a resolution?

A resolution is the main written output of committee debate. It usually includes background/context clauses and action clauses.

Preambulatory clauses

These explain context, principles, and concerns.

Recognizing Acknowledging Deeply concerned Noting with concern Recalling Affirming Emphasizing Guided by

Operative clauses

These state what the committee recommends, encourages, or requests.

Calls upon Encourages Recommends Requests Supports Urges Invites Further requests

Mini example

Clause type Example
Preambulatory Recognizing the disproportionate pressure placed on major refugee-hosting states,
Operative Encourages member states to increase financial and technical support for countries hosting large refugee populations,

Good resolution habits

  • Be specific
  • Use realistic wording
  • Focus on what your committee could plausibly support
  • Write clauses others may actually accept
  • Improve language through negotiation

Weak habits to avoid

  • Very vague moral language
  • Trying to solve everything in one clause
  • Ignoring sovereignty or state capacity
  • Using personal instead of diplomatic wording
  • Writing unrealistic or impossible commitments

Protocol & Committee Flow

Basic student rules

  1. Speak as your assigned country.
  2. Wait to be recognized in formal session.
  3. Stay concise and focused.
  4. Be respectful and diplomatic.
  5. Negotiate during caucusing.
  6. Try to improve wording, not only repeat opinions.

What common committee phrases mean

  • The Chair recognizes... = you may speak now.
  • Caucus = a more flexible discussion/negotiation phase.
  • Amendment = a proposed change to draft text.
  • Vote = committee decision stage.

How to disagree diplomatically

Less effective Better diplomatic phrasing
That idea is wrong. Our delegation has reservations about that proposal.
We reject this completely. Our delegation would have difficulty supporting that language in its current form.
This is unrealistic. Our delegation believes a more practical approach would be...
This hurts our interests. Our delegation is concerned that such a measure may conflict with national capacity or sovereignty considerations.
If you feel nervous: rely on preparation. Use your position paper, your speech notes, and your country’s core interests as your anchor.

FAQ

What if I personally disagree with my country?

That is normal. In MUN, you are practicing representation and analysis, not expressing your private beliefs.

What if I feel nervous about speaking?

Keep your opening speech short, prepare it in advance, and focus on clarity rather than performance. You do not need to be dramatic. You need to be prepared.

What if I do not know enough about my country?

Focus on the agenda, the country’s likely interests, and a few realistic policy positions. No delegate knows everything.

What if my partner is passive?

Divide work clearly. One person can focus more on speaking while the other focuses more on drafting and research, but both should be ready to contribute.

What if I miss a class?

Review the posted materials, communicate with your partner, and use the toolkit and resources app to catch up quickly.

Do I need to sound extremely formal?

No. You should sound respectful and diplomatic, but also clear and natural.
