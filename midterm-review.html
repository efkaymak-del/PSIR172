📋 Exam Format

Closed book. No notes, no devices. Duration: 80 minutes. Total: 20 marks = 20% of your final grade.

Section A — Multiple Choice (8 marks)
8 questions, 1 mark each, four options. Covers Weeks 1–7.
Section B — Short Analysis (4 marks)
Read a short passage. Two questions (2 marks each): identify the framing, identify the persuasion mode. Write 3–5 sentences per answer.
Section C — Applied Scenario (8 marks)
Write a campaign communication plan (300–400 words) for a given scenario. Four parts, 2 marks each: (a) target audience, (b) framing strategy, (c) key message using 2+ persuasion modes, (d) ethical awareness.

💡 How to Prepare

1. Use this guide. Go through every concept — can you explain each one in your own words without looking?

2. Do the practice MCQs in this app. Do them twice.

3. Practise Section C: pick any topic, write a 300-word campaign plan with audience, framing, message, and ethics. Time yourself (40 minutes).

4. Know these key differences: research question vs. recommendation · positive vs. negative framing · ethos vs. pathos vs. logos · correlation vs. causation · policy brief vs. essay · paid vs. earned media.

5. Don't memorise McNair. Understand the ideas.

6. Read exam questions carefully. In Section C, marks are given for each part (a–d). Don't skip any part.

📝 Practice: Section B

Read the passage below, then try to answer the two questions before revealing the model answer.

Passage:

A student organisation publishes the following social media post:

"Did you know that only 12% of students vote in campus elections? Last year, just 340 people decided who runs your student union — a body that controls a €200,000 budget. 'Students deserve leaders they actually chose,' says outgoing president Elif Arslan. This Thursday: vote. Your voice. Your money. Your campus."

Question 1 (2 marks)

Is this passage using positive framing, negative framing, or both? Explain by referring to specific words or phrases.

Model Answer — Question 1

The passage uses both positive and negative framing. It opens with negative framing to create urgency and shame: "only 12% of students vote" and "just 340 people decided" use small numbers to make the reader feel the situation is wrong. The phrase "your student union" implies something is being taken from the reader. It then shifts to positive framing to inspire action: "Students deserve leaders they actually chose" appeals to fairness, and "Your voice. Your money. Your campus" uses empowerment language — giving the reader ownership and agency. The negative frame identifies the problem; the positive frame provides the motivation to act.

Question 2 (2 marks)

Identify which persuasion modes (ethos, pathos, logos) are used. Give one example from the text for each mode you identify.

Model Answer — Question 2

All three modes are present. Logos (evidence): the statistics "12%," "340 people," and "€200,000 budget" provide factual data that supports the argument. Ethos (credibility): the quote is attributed to "outgoing president Elif Arslan," an authority figure within the student community whose position gives her words weight. Pathos (emotion): "Your voice. Your money. Your campus" creates an emotional sense of ownership and urgency — the short, punchy sentences are designed to make the reader feel personally affected.

📝 Practice: Section C

Read the scenario and write your response (300–400 words). Then compare with the model answer. Try writing before you look!

Scenario:

A local animal shelter is struggling. Fewer people are adopting pets, and the shelter is running out of space and funding. The shelter director has asked you to design a short communication campaign to encourage more adoptions.

(a) Target Audience (2 marks)

Who is your campaign aimed at? Choose a specific group and explain why.

(b) Framing Strategy (2 marks)

Positive, negative, or both? Explain your choice and give an example message.

(c) Key Message (2 marks)

Write 3–4 sentences for a poster, social post, or video. Use at least 2 persuasion modes.

(d) Ethical Awareness (2 marks)

Identify one ethical risk and explain how you would avoid it.

Model Answer — Section C

(a) Target Audience: Young adults aged 20–30 living near the shelter, especially university students and young professionals living alone or in shared housing. This group is more likely to have flexible schedules and living situations that allow pet ownership, and they are heavy social media users, making them easy to reach online.

(b) Framing Strategy: Both. Open with negative framing to create urgency: "Every week, the shelter takes in more animals than it can hold. Without new adoptions, healthy animals face being put down." Then shift to positive framing for the solution: "One visit could change two lives — yours and theirs." The negative frame establishes the problem; the positive frame makes the action feel rewarding rather than burdensome.

(c) Key Message: "Right now, 47 dogs and cats at the Famagusta City Shelter are waiting for a home. Each one is healthy, vaccinated, and ready to be part of your family. Dr. Ayşe Polat, the shelter's head veterinarian, says: 'These animals have so much love to give — they just need someone to give them a chance.' Visit us this Saturday. Your new best friend is already here."

This uses logos (47 animals, vaccinated, healthy — concrete facts), ethos (Dr. Polat, head veterinarian — expert credibility), and pathos ("so much love to give," "your new best friend" — emotional connection).

(d) Ethical Risk: The campaign could use guilt-tripping language ("these animals will die because of you") or show distressing images of suffering animals to manipulate emotions. This crosses the line from persuasion into manipulation. To avoid it, I would focus on hopeful, positive images of healthy animals and use factual urgency (shelter capacity numbers) rather than graphic emotional pressure. The message should motivate action through hope, not guilt.
