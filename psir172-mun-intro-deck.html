UN Background

What Is the United Nations?

The United Nations was founded in 1945 and is an international organization created to support cooperation among states.

Its work broadly concerns:

  • international peace and security
  • economic and social development
  • human rights
  • humanitarian cooperation
  • international law and diplomacy
MUN only makes sense if students first understand that the UN is a system of institutions, procedures, and state representation — not just a generic debate club.

This class simulation is simplified for learning, but it is based on real UN logics and structures.

UN Structure

The Main Organs of the UN

General Assembly

Main deliberative and representative body. All 193 member states are represented.

Security Council

Responsible for international peace and security. 15 members.

ECOSOC

Coordinates economic, social, and environmental policy dialogue.

International Court of Justice

Principal judicial organ of the UN, based in The Hague.

Secretariat

Carries out the day-to-day work of the organization.

Trusteeship Council

Historically supervised trust territories; operations suspended in 1994.

Key institutional difference: the General Assembly is universal and representative; the Security Council is smaller and focused on peace and security.
GA vs UNSC

General Assembly vs Security Council

Feature General Assembly Security Council
Membership All 193 UN member states 15 members total
Main function Deliberation, representation, recommendations, broad global issues Primary responsibility for international peace and security
Typical classroom feel Large, multilateral, broad participation Smaller, higher-stakes, concentrated membership
Why it matters for us Fits a full class and broad humanitarian topic Too small to naturally accommodate 36 students equally
Important: We are not doing a UNSC simulation because the real Security Council has only 15 members and does not fit our class size or teaching goals well.
What Is MUN?

What Is Model United Nations?

  • MUN is an academic simulation of UN processes.
  • Students represent countries, not themselves.
  • Delegates research state positions and interests.
  • Delegates debate, negotiate, and draft documents.
  • The goal is not only debate — it is also cooperation and problem-solving.

Core principle

Represent the state, not yourself.

You may be assigned a country whose policies differ from your own views. That is part of the educational value of MUN.

MUN Diversity

MUN Is Not One Single Format

Around the world, MUN conferences use different committee types, sizes, rules, and written outputs. Some simulate broad UN bodies. Others simulate smaller councils, specialized agencies, regional bodies, or crisis scenarios.

So when we say “we are doing MUN”, that does not mean there is only one correct format. The format must be chosen according to:

class size student experience level room constraints topic complexity learning objectives
Committee Types

Common MUN Formats Worldwide

Format Typical style General characteristics
General Assembly Large, resolution-based Broad representation, major global issues, formal debate and resolutions
ECOSOC / Social-Economic Bodies Mid-sized, resolution-based Development, social policy, economic and environmental issues
Regional Bodies Mid-sized Focused on a specific geographic region or organization
Specialized Bodies Smaller, often directive-based Narrower institutional focus, more role-specific negotiation
Crisis Committees Small, fast-paced, directive-based Reactive, dynamic, often more advanced and less beginner-friendly
Alternatives

Why Some Conferences Use UNSC, Specialized, or Crisis Committees

UNSC

  • good for peace and security topics
  • small membership
  • works best with smaller groups

Specialized Bodies

  • good for narrow policy or institutional themes
  • often smaller and more technical
  • can be strong for advanced students

Crisis

  • fast-moving and often unpredictable
  • requires improvisation
  • usually better for experienced delegates
Why Not UNSC?

Why We Are Not Doing a Security Council MUN

  • The real Security Council has 15 members.
  • Our class has about 36 students.
  • We want all students engaged at the same time in the same room.
  • A full UNSC format would leave too many students in artificial or secondary roles.
  • UNSC simulations can be excellent, but they are not automatically the best choice for every class.
  • For our course, a larger representative committee is more inclusive, more practical, and more teachable.
  • This is a deliberate design choice, not an accidental compromise.
Why GA-Style?

Why We Are Using a GA-Style Humanitarian Committee

Practical reasons

  • everyone can participate
  • works in one room
  • fits 18 delegations
  • supports broad international representation

Educational reasons

  • better for first-year students
  • good for learning negotiation and coalition-building
  • suited to consensus-building and drafting
  • appropriate for a broad humanitarian agenda
Our approach: we are simulating a General Assembly–style humanitarian committee, adapted for classroom learning.

Agenda

Our Agenda

International Responsibility-Sharing for Refugees and Displaced Persons

This topic asks how states and the international community should share responsibility for protecting and supporting refugees and displaced persons.

Key dimensions

  • hosting refugees
  • international funding
  • resettlement and burden-sharing
  • border pressures and transit issues

Also relevant

  • humanitarian obligations
  • state capacity
  • regional instability
  • sovereignty and domestic politics
Why This Topic?

Why This Topic Works for Our MUN

Accessible

Students can understand the issue relatively quickly and begin research without highly technical background.

Diverse Positions

Host states, donor states, transit states, destination states, and major powers all face the issue differently.

Strong Diplomacy

The topic creates room for disagreement, bargaining, coalition-building, and resolution drafting.

What Delegates Do

What Delegates Actually Do

Before the simulation

  • research the assigned country
  • identify interests and concerns
  • write a position paper
  • prepare an opening speech

During the simulation

  • speak formally
  • negotiate informally
  • build alliances / blocs
  • draft and revise resolutions
A delegate’s goal is not simply to “win the debate” but to represent the country effectively and work toward an outcome that the country could support.
Position Papers

What Is a Position Paper?

A position paper is a short written statement from the perspective of your assigned country.

  • explains the issue briefly
  • states the country’s position
  • identifies key interests and concerns
  • proposes solutions the country would support

Main point

This is not a personal essay. It is a delegation brief that prepares you for the simulation.

Position Paper Structure

Core Elements of a Good Position Paper

1. The issue

What is the problem under discussion?

2. Country position

How does your country understand the issue?

3. National interests

What concerns shape your state’s view?

4. Policies supported

What principles or actions would your country back?

5. Proposed solutions

What measures would your delegation advocate?

6. Conclusion

What overall message should the committee hear from your delegation?

Research

How to Research Your Country

Ask questions like:

  • Is this country mainly a host, donor, transit, or destination state?
  • What has this government said publicly?
  • What kinds of burden-sharing would it support?
  • What would it probably reject?
  • Which countries might it align with?

Use sources like:

  • UNHCR
  • UN documents and reports
  • government / foreign ministry websites
  • reputable news and policy analysis
  • country voting records or public statements where relevant

Key Takeaway

Final Takeaway

The simulation must be designed first.
Your role is assigned next.
Your position paper prepares you to perform that role.

In other words:

  • we are not writing position papers in the abstract
  • we are preparing to represent specific countries
  • the paper is the bridge between research and simulation
