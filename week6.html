🎯 Target Audience Analysis

Every message has an intended receiver. Before you write or speak, answer three questions about your audience:

1. What do they already believe? You're not starting from zero — your audience has existing views. Work with them or deliberately challenge them.

2. What do they care about? A finance minister cares about costs. An NGO director cares about rights. Frame your message around their priorities, not yours.

3. What would move them to act? Information alone rarely works. You need an emotional hook — urgency, moral obligation, political opportunity — that connects evidence to action.

For your policy brief: Your brief is addressed to a specific decision-maker. Name them. Then rewrite your executive summary as if that person is the only reader. Does it speak to what they care about?

🎭 Emotional Framing: Ethos, Pathos, Logos

Ethos = credibility. Why should the audience trust you? Use credentials, cite authoritative sources, demonstrate expertise.

Pathos = emotion. Why should the audience care? Use vivid examples, human stories, striking data. Pathos gets people to read past the first paragraph.

Logos = logic and evidence. Why should the audience believe your argument? Use data, research, cause-and-effect reasoning.

Example: "200,000 refugees crossed the Mediterranean last year" is logos. "A three-year-old boy washed up on a beach in Turkey" is pathos. Both are about the same crisis. One changed European policy overnight.

⚖️ Positive vs. Negative Framing

The same policy can be sold in two completely different ways:

Positive Frame Emotion: Hope, opportunity
Logic: "If we do X, we gain Y"
Best for: Inspiring action, building coalitions
Example: "Investing in renewable energy will create 50,000 new jobs."
Negative Frame Emotion: Fear, urgency
Logic: "If we don't do X, we lose Y"
Best for: Mobilising against threats, creating urgency
Example: "If we don't act on climate change, coastal cities will flood within 30 years."

Research shows negative frames grab attention faster, but positive frames sustain engagement longer. Good policy briefs use both: negative to open (the problem), positive to close (the recommendation).

📦 The Message Box

A tool used by political campaigns to map out messaging strategy. It forces you to think about what the other side will say — not just your own argument.

What WE say about US
What WE say about THEM
What THEY say about THEM
"We promote jobs and growth"
"They ignore environmental costs"
What THEY say about US
"They say we're idealistic — but the evidence is on our side"
"They claim to care about workers but oppose renewable investment"
